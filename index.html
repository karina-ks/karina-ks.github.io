Hi, I'm Karina!

I'm an astronomy PhD student at Georgia State University interested in the stars, planets, and planetary systems that make up our Galaxy.

My research focuses on exoplanets around M dwarfs, stellar rotation, and stellar activity. I'm particularly interested in understanding what the outer architecture of M-dwarf planetary systems looks like — and whether the planets we have found so far are actually representative of the systems that exist.

A big part of my work is thinking about observational bias: sometimes "we haven't found it" and "it isn't there" are two very different statements.

Outside of research, I enjoy finding ways to make astronomy a little less intimidating and a lot more fun to talk about.

Research

I'm interested in the planets we find, the planets we don't find, and the reasons those two things might not be the same.

  • Exoplanets & M Dwarfs

    M dwarfs are the most common stars in the Galaxy, but the planetary systems we have discovered around them appear to be dominated by planets on relatively short orbits. I want to understand what the outer architecture of these systems really looks like.

  • Stellar Rotation

    I study stellar rotation in M dwarfs and use photometric observations to investigate how these stars rotate and evolve. Rotation also provides an important window into stellar magnetic activity.

  • Observational Bias

    Planet searches are not equally sensitive to every kind of planetary system. Stellar activity can make planets harder to detect, so I'm interested in finding ways to look for systems that traditional searches may overlook.

One question that keeps coming back in my work is:

Are there truly no planets around the fastest rotators?

Or are those systems simply harder for us to find?

Answering that distinction is important if we want exoplanet demographics to tell us about the planets that actually exist — rather than only the planets that happen to be easiest to detect.

Lingo Catalog

Because astronomy has approximately twelve thousand words that sound much more complicated than they need to be.

Astronomy comes with its own language, and sometimes the hardest part of learning a new concept is figuring out what everyone is actually talking about.

The Lingo Catalog is my collection of astronomy terms, concepts, and explanations — especially the ones that make much more sense when someone explains them like a normal human being.
